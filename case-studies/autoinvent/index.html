AutoInvent is an AI patent filing platform for founders and inventors. Over the summer, I worked directly with founders and engineers in weekly product decisions to redesign core workflows across invention, reinvention, and patent filing.

The Problem

Patent workflows are high-stakes, unfamiliar, and easy to overwhelm with AI.

AutoInvent was helping users do several hard things at once: discover patents, reinvent ideas, draft filings, and monitor competitors. The challenge was trust and agency! Users needed strong guidance without feeling like AI was taking over their invention.

Three tensions kept surfacing:

1

Core product architecture was fragmented across Dashboard, My Ideas, and filing states.

2

AI suggestions could feel too final, weakening user ownership.

3

Users struggled to stay engaged when patent discovery felt too broad and unstructured.

The Solution

How might we help users navigate AI-assisted patenting without losing clarity, ownership, or momentum?

1. Unifying workflow architecture

Merged Dashboard and My Ideas into a unified system that matches users' mental model: idea, draft, filed.

2. Making AI feel steerable

Redesigned the ReInvent flow so AI suggestions felt like starting points users could shape and stay in control over.

3. Designing for retention

An abandoned-patent browsing experience that drives an ongoing cycle of discovery, engagement, and reinvention.

My process was lightweight and iterative, shaped by the pace of an early-stage startup. I moved between user feedback, product conversations, and rapid design iteration, grounding decisions in 15+ user sessions, competitor patterns, and close collaboration with founders and engineers.

Design Principles

Every principle was a tradeoff. Here's where I landed!

Decision 1: Restructuring around user progress

Users’ work was split across Dashboard and My Ideas, making it unclear where something lived or what stage it was in.

Decision 2: Making AI assistance feel steerable

In the original ReInvent flow (for revamping abandoned patents), AI suggestions felt too much like preset answers. In a product centered on originality and ownership, that made the system feel overly authoritative.

Designing with AI

AI changed how quickly I could do design work, but also, the kind of work I could do! I used AI-assisted tooling to generate feature variants and accelerate exploration, but the key decision remained as how much control users should have, when AI should intervene, and how to stay people oriented in an information-heavy workflow.

Takeaways!

This project sharpened what I care most about as a designer: designing AI systems that support people without taking over; structuring ambiguous workflows into clear interaction flows; and reducing cognitive load through product logic.

It also taught me that the most important AI product decisions are often about sequencing trust — what the system asks first, what it explains, when it offers help, and how it keeps the user feeling like the work is still theirs. That’s the kind of design work I want to keep doing.
